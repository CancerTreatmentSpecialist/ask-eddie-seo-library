Cancer Staging Explained

Reviewed by Dr. Derrick J. Beech, MD, FACS, Surgical Oncologist
Last updated: September 2026

Cancer staging describes how much cancer is in the body, where it is located, and whether it has spread. Staging is one of the most important parts of diagnosis because it determines treatment options and helps predict outcomes.

Why Staging Matters

  • How advanced is the cancer?
  • Has it spread to lymph nodes?
  • Has it spread to other organs?
  • What treatments are recommended?
  • What is the expected prognosis?

Staging guides decisions about surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy.

The TNM System (Most Common Worldwide)

The TNM system is used for most solid tumors.

T — Tumor

Describes the size and extent of the main tumor. T1: small tumor. T2: medium. T3: larger or growing into nearby tissue. T4: very large or invading nearby structures.

N — Nodes

Describes whether cancer has spread to nearby lymph nodes. N0: no lymph node involvement. N1: a few nearby nodes. N2–N3: more or distant nodes.

M — Metastasis

Describes whether cancer has spread to distant organs. M0: no metastasis. M1: cancer has spread to distant organs (lungs, liver, bones, brain).

Stage I–IV (Simplified System Patients See Most Often)

After TNM is determined, doctors assign an overall stage:

Stage I — Early Cancer

Small tumor, no lymph node involvement, no spread. Often highly treatable.

Stage II — Larger or Deeper Tumor

May involve nearby tissue, usually no lymph node involvement. Treatment often includes surgery plus radiation or systemic therapy.

Stage III — Locally Advanced Cancer

Larger tumor, lymph node involvement, no distant spread. Often requires combination therapy (surgery, radiation, chemotherapy).

Stage IV — Metastatic Cancer

Cancer has spread to distant organs. Treatment focuses on systemic therapy — see chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy.

Other Staging Systems

Blood Cancers (Leukemia, Lymphoma)

These use different systems because they don't form solid tumors — for example, Ann Arbor staging (lymphoma) and Rai/Binet staging (leukemia).

Brain Tumors

Often graded instead of staged because they rarely spread outside the brain.

Gynecologic Cancers

Use FIGO staging (cervical, ovarian, uterine).

Key Terms Patients Often Find Confusing

  • Localized — cancer is only in the original area
  • Regional — cancer has spread to nearby lymph nodes or tissues
  • Distant — cancer has spread to other organs (metastatic)
  • In situ — very early cancer that has not invaded deeper tissue
  • Invasive — cancer has grown beyond its original layer
