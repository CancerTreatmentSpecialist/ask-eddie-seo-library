Margins and Grade in Cancer Pathology

Margins and grade are two of the most important parts of a pathology report. They help determine how aggressive a cancer is and whether surgery successfully removed all visible disease. Understanding these terms empowers patients to make informed decisions about treatment.

---

What “Margins” Mean

Margins describe the edges of the tissue removed during surgery. Pathologists examine these edges under a microscope to see whether cancer cells are present.

Types of Margins

• Negative (Clear) Margins
No cancer cells at the edge of the removed tissue.
This is the ideal result — it means the surgeon removed all visible cancer.
• Positive Margins
Cancer cells are found at the edge.
This suggests some cancer may remain and additional treatment may be needed.
• Close Margins
Cancer cells are very near the edge (often within 1–2 mm).
This may increase the risk of recurrence depending on the cancer type.


Why Margins Matter

Margins help determine whether a patient needs:

• more surgery
• radiation therapy
• chemotherapy
• closer follow-up


Margins are especially important in breast, colorectal, head and neck, and skin cancers.

---

What “Grade” Means

Grade describes how abnormal the cancer cells look under the microscope. It reflects how quickly the cancer is likely to grow and spread.

Grades Explained

• Low Grade (Grade 1)
Cells look more like normal cells.
Slower-growing, less aggressive.
• Intermediate Grade (Grade 2)
Cells look moderately abnormal.
Medium growth rate.
• High Grade (Grade 3)
Cells look very abnormal.
Faster-growing, more aggressive.


Why Grade Matters

Grade helps determine:

• treatment intensity
• likelihood of recurrence
• need for chemotherapy or radiation
• prognosis


High‑grade cancers often require more aggressive treatment.

---

Related Terms You May See

Differentiation

How closely cancer cells resemble normal cells.

• Well‑differentiated = low grade
• Poorly differentiated = high grade


Mitotic Rate / Ki‑67

Measures how quickly cancer cells are dividing.
Higher numbers mean faster growth.

Lymphovascular Invasion (LVI)

Cancer cells found in blood vessels or lymph channels.
This increases the risk of spread.

---

How Margins and Grade Work Together

Margins tell whether cancer was fully removed.
Grade tells how aggressive the cancer is.

Examples:

• High‑grade cancer + positive margins
→ often requires additional surgery and systemic therapy.
• Low‑grade cancer + clear margins
→ may only need routine follow-up.
• Intermediate grade + close margins
→ may require radiation to reduce recurrence risk.
