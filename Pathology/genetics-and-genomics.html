Genetics & Genomics in Cancer

Reviewed by Dr. Derrick J. Beech, MD, FACS, Surgical Oncologist
Last updated: September 2026

Genetics and genomics play a major role in understanding how cancer develops, how it behaves, and which treatments will work best. Cancer is fundamentally a disease of DNA changes — these changes can increase cancer risk, drive cancer growth, determine how aggressive a tumor is, predict which treatments will work, and guide immunotherapy and targeted therapy decisions. Understanding a patient's genetic and genomic profile is now a core part of modern oncology.

Genetics vs. Genomics — What's the Difference?

Genetics (Germline DNA)

Inherited DNA you are born with. These mutations are present in every cell of the body. Examples include BRCA1/BRCA2, Lynch syndrome genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2), HOXB13 (prostate cancer risk), and TP53 (Li-Fraumeni syndrome). Germline testing helps identify cancer risk and guides screening and family counseling.

Genomics (Somatic DNA)

DNA changes found only in the tumor. These mutations are not inherited — they develop over time. Examples include EGFR (lung cancer), KRAS (colorectal, lung), BRAF (melanoma, thyroid), PIK3CA (breast cancer), and HER2 amplification (breast, gastric). Genomic testing helps determine treatment options.

Types of Genetic and Genomic Testing

Germline Testing (Inherited Risk)

Used to identify hereditary cancer syndromes, often done through blood or saliva. Common panels include BRCA1/2, Lynch syndrome genes, TP53, PALB2, and CHEK2.

Somatic Tumor Testing (Genomic Profiling)

Analyzes DNA from the tumor itself, often done through biopsy tissue or liquid biopsy. Tests look for mutations, amplifications, deletions, fusions, and expression markers, which guide targeted therapy and immunotherapy.

Key Genomic Markers in Cancer

  • EGFR — targeted therapy for lung cancer
  • KRAS — common mutation in colorectal and lung cancer; affects treatment choices
  • BRAF — important in melanoma, thyroid, and colorectal cancer
  • HER2 — determines eligibility for HER2-targeted therapy
  • PD-L1 — predicts response to immunotherapy
  • MSI-H / MMR deficiency — indicates strong response to immunotherapy; linked to Lynch syndrome
  • PIK3CA — guides targeted therapy in breast cancer

How Genomic Testing Helps Patients

  • Targeted therapy — drugs that attack specific mutations (e.g., EGFR inhibitors, HER2 therapies)
  • Immunotherapy decisions — markers like PD-L1 and MSI-H help determine whether immunotherapy will work
  • Prognosis — some mutations indicate more aggressive disease
  • Treatment avoidance — certain mutations show when chemotherapy may not be needed
  • Family risk assessment — germline mutations help identify risk for relatives

Common Patient Confusions

Patients often struggle with the difference between inherited vs. tumor mutations, why some mutations matter and others don't, why genomic testing takes time, why results differ between labs, what "variant of uncertain significance" means, and how mutations affect treatment choices.
