Liquid Biopsy

Reviewed by Dr. Derrick J. Beech, MD, FACS, Surgical Oncologist
Last updated: September 2026

A liquid biopsy is a blood test that looks for cancer DNA, RNA, or proteins circulating in the bloodstream. It is a powerful tool for detecting tumor mutations, monitoring treatment, and identifying recurrence — all without needing a traditional tissue biopsy.

What a Liquid Biopsy Is

A liquid biopsy analyzes circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), circulating tumor cells (CTCs), RNA fragments, and tumor-related proteins. These fragments enter the bloodstream when cancer cells die or shed material. Liquid biopsy is minimally invasive, repeatable, and useful when tissue biopsy is difficult.

Why Liquid Biopsy Matters

  • Does the tumor have mutations that can be targeted?
  • Is treatment working?
  • Has the cancer returned?
  • Is the cancer changing over time?
  • Are new mutations emerging that affect therapy?

It complements — but does not replace — tissue biopsy.

What Liquid Biopsy Can Detect

1. Tumor Mutations (Genomic Profiling)

Examples include EGFR, KRAS, BRAF, PIK3CA, ALK fusions, and HER2 amplification — see Genetics & Genomics. These guide targeted therapy decisions.

2. Minimal Residual Disease (MRD)

Detects tiny amounts of cancer after treatment. Useful in colorectal, breast, lung, and blood cancers.

3. Treatment Resistance

Shows when new mutations appear that make therapy less effective.

4. Recurrence

ctDNA can rise months before imaging shows recurrence.

When Liquid Biopsy Is Used

Diagnosis (When Tissue Is Hard to Access)

Examples include lung cancer, liver cancer, pancreatic cancer, metastatic disease, and frail patients who cannot undergo biopsy.

Treatment Planning

To identify mutations for targeted therapy.

Monitoring Treatment

Falling ctDNA generally means treatment is working; rising ctDNA suggests possible progression.

Surveillance After Treatment

Detects recurrence early.

Limitations of Liquid Biopsy

Liquid biopsy is powerful but not perfect: very small tumors may not shed enough DNA, not all mutations appear in blood, false negatives can occur, tissue biopsy is still required for full diagnosis, and results vary between labs. Liquid biopsy is a complement, not a replacement.

Common Patient Confusions

Patients often struggle with the difference between ctDNA and tumor DNA, why liquid biopsy cannot always replace tissue biopsy, why results differ between labs, why some mutations appear in blood but not tissue, and why oncologists repeat liquid biopsy over time.
