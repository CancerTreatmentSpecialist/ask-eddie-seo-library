Understanding Your Pathology Report

A pathology report explains what was found in a biopsy or surgical sample. It is one of the most important documents in cancer care — guiding diagnosis, treatment decisions, and prognosis. This page breaks down the key sections in plain language so patients can understand what their report means.

---

What a Pathology Report Is

A pathology report is written by a pathologist, a doctor who examines tissue under a microscope.
The report describes:

• what the cells look like
• whether cancer is present
• how aggressive the cancer appears
• whether it has spread
• which markers or mutations are found


This information shapes the entire treatment plan.

---

Main Sections of a Pathology Report

1. Patient Information

Basic details such as name, date of birth, and medical record number.

2. Specimen Description

What tissue was removed and how it was obtained.
Examples:

• breast core biopsy
• colon polyp
• lymph node excision
• lung needle biopsy


3. Clinical History

Why the biopsy was done — symptoms, imaging findings, or previous results.

4. Gross Description

What the sample looks like to the naked eye: size, color, texture.
This section is often technical and not essential for patients.

5. Microscopic Description

What the cells look like under the microscope.
This is where cancer is confirmed or ruled out.

6. Diagnosis (Final Diagnosis)

The most important part of the report.
It may include:

• cancer type
• grade
• margins
• invasion
• lymph node involvement
• receptor status
• mutations


7. Additional Studies

Tests such as:

• ER/PR/HER2 (breast cancer)
• Ki‑67 (growth rate)
• PD‑L1 (immunotherapy response)
• genetic mutations (EGFR, KRAS, BRAF, BRCA)


8. Comments

Pathologists may add clarifications or recommendations.

---

Key Terms Explained

Carcinoma / Adenocarcinoma / Sarcoma

These words describe the type of cancer based on the cells involved.

Invasive vs. In Situ

• Invasive: cancer has spread into surrounding tissue
• In situ: early cancer that has not spread


Grade (Low, Intermediate, High)

How aggressive the cancer cells look.
High‑grade cancers grow and spread faster.

Margins

Edges of the removed tissue.

• Negative margins: no cancer at the edges
• Positive margins: cancer reaches the edge → may need more surgery


Lymphovascular Invasion (LVI)

Cancer cells found in blood vessels or lymph channels.
This increases the risk of spread.

Mitotic Rate / Ki‑67

Measures how quickly cancer cells are dividing.

Receptor Status

Important for breast cancer:

• ER (estrogen receptor)
• PR (progesterone receptor)
• HER2


These guide treatment options.

Genetic Mutations

Mutations can determine whether targeted therapy will work.

---

Why Pathology Reports Are Hard to Understand

Patients often struggle because reports use:

• complex medical terminology
• long sentences
• Latin‑based cancer names
• abbreviations
• microscopic descriptions
• technical measurements


This is normal — pathology reports are written for oncologists, not patients.
