Genetics and Genomics in Cancer

Genetics and genomics play a major role in understanding how cancer develops, how it behaves, and which treatments will work best. This page explains the difference between inherited (germline) and acquired (somatic) mutations, how genomic testing works, and why these results matter for cancer care.

---

Why Genetics and Genomics Matter

Cancer is fundamentally a disease of DNA changes. These changes can:

• increase cancer risk
• drive cancer growth
• determine how aggressive a tumor is
• predict which treatments will work
• guide immunotherapy and targeted therapy decisions


Understanding a patient’s genetic and genomic profile is now a core part of modern oncology.

---

Genetics vs. Genomics — What’s the Difference?

Genetics (Germline DNA)

Inherited DNA you are born with.
These mutations are present in every cell of the body.

Examples of germline mutations:

• BRCA1 / BRCA2
• Lynch syndrome genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2)
• HOXB13 (prostate cancer risk)
• TP53 (Li‑Fraumeni syndrome)


Germline testing helps identify cancer risk and guides screening and family counseling.

Genomics (Somatic DNA)

DNA changes found only in the tumor.
These mutations are not inherited — they develop over time.

Examples of somatic mutations:

• EGFR (lung cancer)
• KRAS (colorectal, lung)
• BRAF (melanoma, thyroid)
• PIK3CA (breast cancer)
• HER2 amplification (breast, gastric)


Genomic testing helps determine treatment options.

---

Types of Genetic and Genomic Testing

Germline Testing (Inherited Risk)

Used to identify hereditary cancer syndromes.
Often done through blood or saliva.

Common panels include:

• BRCA1/2
• Lynch syndrome genes
• TP53
• PALB2
• CHEK2


Somatic Tumor Testing (Genomic Profiling)

Analyzes DNA from the tumor itself.
Often done through biopsy tissue or liquid biopsy.

Tests look for:

• mutations
• amplifications
• deletions
• fusions
• expression markers


These results guide targeted therapy and immunotherapy.

Key Genomic Markers in Cancer

EGFR

Targeted therapy for lung cancer.

KRAS

Common mutation in colorectal and lung cancer; affects treatment choices.

BRAF

Important in melanoma, thyroid, and colorectal cancer.

HER2

Determines eligibility for HER2‑targeted therapy.

PD‑L1

Predicts response to immunotherapy.

MSI‑H / MMR Deficiency

Indicates strong response to immunotherapy; linked to Lynch syndrome.

PIK3CA

Guides targeted therapy in breast cancer.

How Genomic Testing Helps Patients

1. Targeted Therapy

Drugs that attack specific mutations (e.g., EGFR inhibitors, HER2 therapies).

2. Immunotherapy Decisions

Markers like PD‑L1 and MSI‑H help determine whether immunotherapy will work.

3. Prognosis

Some mutations indicate more aggressive disease.

4. Treatment Avoidance

Certain mutations show when chemotherapy may not be needed.

5. Family Risk Assessment

Germline mutations help identify risk for relatives.
