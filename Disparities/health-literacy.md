Health Literacy and Cancer Outcomes

Health literacy — the ability to find, understand, and use health information — is one of the strongest predictors of cancer outcomes. Low health literacy leads to delayed diagnosis, reduced treatment completion, and poorer survivorship.

---

Overview

Many patients struggle to understand medical terms, screening instructions, pathology reports, and treatment plans. These gaps are not the patient’s fault — they reflect systemic communication barriers, rushed appointments, and complex healthcare environments.

Low health literacy disproportionately affects Indigenous peoples, Black communities, rural residents, newcomers, and low‑income households.

---

What Low Health Literacy Looks Like

• Difficulty understanding medical terms
• Confusion about screening instructions
• Not knowing which symptoms require follow-up
• Trouble navigating referrals
• Misunderstanding treatment side effects
• Feeling intimidated or rushed during appointments
• Not asking questions due to fear or shame


---

Impact on Cancer Outcomes

Low health literacy contributes to:

• Later-stage diagnosis
• Missed screening appointments
• Delayed follow-up after abnormal results
• Lower treatment completion
• Reduced access to advanced therapies
• Higher emergency visits
• Poorer survivorship support


---

Communities Most Affected

• Indigenous peoples — historical trauma, cultural safety gaps
• Black communities — communication bias, mistrust
• Rural residents — limited access to specialists
• Immigrants and newcomers — language and system navigation barriers
• Low-income households — fewer primary care connections
• Seniors — cognitive and mobility challenges


---

Drivers of Low Health Literacy

• Complex medical language
• Rushed appointments
• Provider bias
• Limited culturally safe communication
• Lack of plain-language materials
• Limited access to primary care
• Language barriers
• Fear of asking questions
• Trauma and mistrust


---

High‑Impact Cancer Areas

Low health literacy strongly affects:

• Screening — mammograms, colonoscopies, Pap tests, lung CT
• Diagnosis — understanding pathology reports and imaging
• Treatment — chemotherapy, radiation, surgery instructions
• Side effects — knowing what is normal vs. dangerous
• Survivorship — follow-up schedules, lifestyle guidance


---

Related Barriers

• provider bias
• transportation barriers
• financial barriers
• specialist access
• trial disparities


---

Evidence‑Based Solutions

• Plain-language communication
• Teach-back method (“Can you tell me in your own words…”)
• Culturally safe education materials
• Visual aids and diagrams
• Longer appointment times for complex decisions
• Community health workers and navigators
• Language translation and interpretation
• Trauma-informed communication
• Clear follow-up instructions
