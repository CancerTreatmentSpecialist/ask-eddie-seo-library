Breast Cancer Screening

Reviewed by Dr. Derrick J. Beech, MD, FACS, Surgical Oncologist
Last updated: September 2026

Breast cancer screening helps detect cancer early — often before symptoms appear — when treatment is most effective. This page explains screening guidelines, tools, risk factors, and common patient questions in clear, accessible language.

Why Breast Cancer Screening Matters

Early detection dramatically improves outcomes: cancers found early are easier to treat, survival rates are significantly higher, fewer patients require aggressive therapy, and screening reduces mortality.

Who Should Be Screened?

Ages 40–49

Screening every 1–2 years based on personal risk and shared decision-making.

Ages 50–74

Routine mammograms every 2 years (or annually depending on jurisdiction).

Ages 75+

Screening based on overall health and life expectancy.

High-Risk Individuals

May need earlier and more frequent screening, including MRI. High-risk includes BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations, strong family history, prior chest radiation, dense breasts, and certain genetic syndromes.

Screening Tools

  • Mammogram — primary screening tool; detects tumors before they can be felt
  • Breast MRI — used for high-risk patients; more sensitive than mammography
  • Breast ultrasound — used when mammograms are unclear or for dense breast tissue
  • Clinical breast exam — performed by a healthcare provider
  • Self-awareness — knowing what is normal for your breasts and reporting changes

Dense Breasts

Dense breast tissue makes mammograms harder to read and increases cancer risk. Patients with dense breasts may benefit from supplemental ultrasound, MRI (if high-risk), or more frequent screening.

Common Findings

Benign: cysts, fibroadenomas, calcifications.
Suspicious: masses, architectural distortion, concerning calcifications.

BI-RADS Explained

  • 0 — needs more imaging
  • 1 — normal
  • 2 — benign
  • 3 — probably benign; short-term follow-up
  • 4 — suspicious; biopsy recommended
  • 5 — highly suspicious
  • 6 — known cancer

High-Risk Screening Programs

Programs often include MRI and genetic counseling for BRCA carriers, those with strong family history, Indigenous patients, dense breast tissue, or prior chest radiation.
