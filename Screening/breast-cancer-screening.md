
# Breast Cancer Screening
Breast cancer screening helps detect cancer early — often before symptoms appear — when treatment is most effective. This page explains screening guidelines, tools, risk factors, and common patient questions in clear, accessible language.

## Why Breast Cancer Screening Matters
Early detection dramatically improves outcomes:
- cancers found early are easier to treat
- survival rates are significantly higher
- fewer patients require aggressive therapy
- screening reduces mortality

## Who Should Be Screened?
### Ages 40–49
Screening every 1–2 years based on personal risk and shared decision-making.

### Ages 50–74
Routine mammograms every 2 years (or annually depending on jurisdiction).

### Ages 75+
Screening based on overall health and life expectancy.

### High-Risk Individuals
May need earlier and more frequent screening, including MRI.

High-risk includes:
- BRCA1/BRCA2 mutations
- strong family history
- prior chest radiation
- dense breasts
- certain genetic syndromes

## Screening Tools
### Mammogram
Primary screening tool; detects tumors before they can be felt.

### Breast MRI
Used for high-risk patients; more sensitive than mammography.

### Breast Ultrasound
Used when mammograms are unclear or for dense breast tissue.

### Clinical Breast Exam
Performed by a healthcare provider.

### Self-Awareness
Knowing what is normal for your breasts and reporting changes.

## Dense Breasts
Dense breast tissue makes mammograms harder to read and increases cancer risk.

Patients with dense breasts may benefit from:
- supplemental ultrasound
- MRI (if high-risk)
- more frequent screening

## Common Findings
### Benign
- cysts
- fibroadenomas
- calcifications

### Suspicious
- masses
- architectural distortion
- concerning calcifications

## BI-RADS Explained
- 0 — needs more imaging
- 1 — normal
- 2 — benign
- 3 — probably benign; short-term follow-up
- 4 — suspicious; biopsy recommended
- 5 — highly suspicious
- 6 — known cancer

## Screening Barriers
- transportation barriers
- financial barriers
- provider bias
- health literacy gaps
- fear or anxiety
- cultural safety concerns
- limited access in rural or remote areas

## High-Risk Screening Programs
Programs often include MRI and genetic counseling for:
- BRCA carriers
- strong family history
- Indigenous patients
- dense breast tissue
- prior chest radiation
